01. Thesis overview

Particle-laden flows are multiscale systems: mechanical interactions take place at the particle scale, while the ensemble develops spatial structure and transport behaviour over larger scales. The thesis asks how finite-size hard-particle collisions modify a distribution already organised by inertia and the carrier flow.

I built a force-free event-scheduled hard-particle reference and a forced kick–drift solver coupling continuous Stokes relaxation to chronological collision handling. The analysis progresses from alternating sinusoidal shear to an experimentally motivated reduced model using time-resolved, near-wall carrier fields reconstructed from turbulent-channel direct numerical simulation (DNS).

Unifying idea: carrier-imposed organisation → collision-induced redistribution. The effects depend on regime, orientation and observation scale.

02. Numerical methods

The numerical and statistical framework separates particle mechanics, carrier-field reconstruction, controlled comparisons and interpretation.

Event-scheduled reference

Force-free hard-disk contacts, collision prediction and chronology, with conservation and consistency checks.

Forced kick–drift solver

Operator splitting couples Stokes-drag relaxation with hard-particle collisions in ballistic drifts; timestep refinement assesses convergence.

Flow configurations

Prescribed alternating sinusoidal shear and a reconstructed time-resolved wall-parallel plane of turbulent-channel DNS.

Statistical analysis

Number fluctuations, autocorrelations, pair anisotropy, collision selection, repeated contacts and uncertainty estimates.

04. Principal findings

  • Regime-dependent clustering in controlled flows. In alternating sinusoidal shear, elastic collisions may suppress strong organisation at high inertia but create spatial inhomogeneity at low inertia; collision effects cannot be described by restitution alone.
  • Carrier structure remains fundamental near the wall. The streamwise organisation of particle populations reflects the underlying streak field, while contacts redistribute the particles in a scale-dependent manner.
  • Distinct effects on structure. In the focused near-wall case, collisions tend to reduce particle-number fluctuations and short-range pair-axis alignment while increasing streamwise density coherence.
  • Repeated contacts do not establish caging. Some pairs repeatedly collide at small separation even as the surrounding population of neighbours changes more rapidly.

The thesis ties these findings together through controlled matched simulations and collision-conditioned statistics, rather than treating every observed cluster as an effect of collisions.

05. Scope and limitations

The near-wall analysis uses a reduced, two-dimensional wall-parallel, one-way-coupled particle model driven by a prescribed DNS-derived carrier record. Particles are finite-sized mechanically, but hydrodynamically unresolved; the model does not solve a fully coupled three-dimensional particle-resolved Navier–Stokes problem.

The detailed near-wall quantitative interpretation focuses on the reported particle-response-time case and its model assumptions. Wall-normal motion, hydrodynamic interactions, lubrication, surface roughness and frictional/rotational contact are outside the baseline study. These boundaries are important for interpreting the findings accurately.

06. Future directions

Stochastic & continuum closure

Reduced population descriptions and collision-history closure from particle dynamics.

Contact-model extensions

Rotation, tangential restitution, friction and eventually wall-normal dynamics.

Learning-based optimisation

Particle transport control and inverse design using data-driven optimisation.
